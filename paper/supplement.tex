\section{Pillar angles}\label{sec:rigangles}

The dataset's file names number the cameras from 1 to \camerasTotal, \camerasPerPillar\ to a pillar, so pillar A
holds cameras 1--\camerasPerPillar. The dataset's label files, which hold the polygons, name the lower legs by the subject's own right and left. The leg offset,
in units of the subject's height in pixels, is the mean horizontal coordinate of the right lower-leg polygon's
vertices minus that of the left lower-leg polygon's vertices. A camera at the angle $\theta$ from the subject's front sees the leg offset
shortened to $\cos\theta$ times its length. For each camera, we took the median of the leg offset over subjects in the measurement
suit's standing photographs. For each pillar, we averaged the cameras' medians over the pillar's cameras. The leg
offset is negative in pillar A, which means that the subject's right leg appears on the left of the image, so pillar A
faces the subject's front. By the $\cos\theta$ shortening, the ratio of a pillar's leg offset to pillar A's leg offset
is about 0.71 at $\pm45^\circ$, 0 at $\pm90^\circ$, $-0.71$ at $\pm135^\circ$ and $-1$ at $180^\circ$.
Figure~\ref{M-fig:rig}(a) in the main text prints each pillar's measured ratio, which is closer to the ratio for the angle at which the main text places the
pillar than to the ratio for any other of these angles.

\section{Shoes}\label{sec:shoes}

To test whether the subjects wore shoes, we paired each own-outfit photograph of a subject with the same camera's
photograph of the subject in the measurement suit. For each pair, we divided the height in pixels in the outfit by the
height in pixels in the suit. For each own outfit, we took the median of this ratio over the pairs. Over the own
outfits, the height in pixels rises from the suit by a median of \statureGainMin--\statureGainMax\%, whereas the
recorded heel height has a median of \heelShareMedian\% of stature.

\section{Waist widths}\label{sec:widths}

The labelers drew the torso in at most \torsoSideMax\% of pillar C's and pillar G's standing photographs and in at
least \torsoOtherMin\% of each other pillar's standing photographs. The torso polygon reaches the level that the hip height
locates in \hipShareMin--\hipShareMax\% of pillar A's standing photographs of each outfit, a range over the outfits. Pillar E gives a half-width in only \footImagesE\ of the \imagesE\
standing photographs that the label files hold for it, because almost none of them carries a foot polygon, so $a$ rests almost entirely on pillar A.

Seen at the angle $\theta$ from the front, the ellipse of the main text, with the semi-axes $a$ and $b$, has the
half-width
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{a^2\cos^2\theta + b^2\sin^2\theta}.
\end{equation}
At $\theta = 45^\circ$, the
half-width $m$ therefore satisfies $2m^2 = a^2 + b^2$, so $b = \sqrt{2m^2 - a^2}$. The ellipse girth is $S$ times
the mean, over the kept camera heights, of Ramanujan's approximation to the perimeter of the ellipse with semi-axes
$a$ and $b$,\cite{ramanujan1914}
\begin{equation}
\pi\bigl[3(a + b) - \sqrt{(3a + b)(a + 3b)}\bigr].
\end{equation}


The kept camera heights also decide which subjects have widths. A subject has widths in an outfit when at least one of the subject's camera heights in that outfit is kept. Under the
primary scale rule, \nSuitSubjects\ subjects have widths in the measurement suit and
\nOwnSubjectsMin--\nOwnSubjectsMax\ in each own outfit.

\section{Self-reported stature and weight}\label{sec:selfreport}

Because a customer reports stature and weight with error, we added self-report errors to stature and weight in
\nDraws\ random draws and repeated the main variant's baseline and width models in each draw. The errors are
normal, with the standard deviations of \selfReportSdCm~cm and \selfReportSdKg~kg that Bartol et al.\
assumed.\cite{bartol2022} In each draw, the stature with its error also sets the scale $S$. With the errors added, the
baseline model's MAPE rises from \mapeBaseline\% to \selfBaseMin--\selfBaseMax\% over the draws. In the same
draws, the widths lower the baseline model's MAPE by \selfRedSuitMin--\selfRedSuitMax~pp in the suit and by
\selfRedOwnMin--\selfRedOwnMax~pp in the own outfits (Table~\ref{tab:selfreport}).

\section{Nonlinear models}\label{sec:nonlinear}

A nonlinear model might draw more from the own outfits' widths than ordinary least squares does. We therefore
repeated the main variant's baseline and width models with a second-order polynomial regression on the standardized
inputs and with gradient-boosted trees, a sum of many small threshold rules on the inputs fitted one after another to the
remaining errors (scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingRegressor at its default settings\cite{pedregosa2011}), on the
same subjects, folds and bootstrap resamples (Table~\ref{tab:nonlinear}). Under both models, the own outfits lose most of the suit's reduction, as they do under ordinary least squares.
